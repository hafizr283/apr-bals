\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

APR-BALS draws on four lines of research: matrix factorization for collaborative filtering, GPU factorization systems, tensor-core and mixed-precision computing, and batched small-matrix solvers. Table~\ref{tab:litgap} summarizes how representative work covers the dimensions this paper combines.

\subsection{Matrix Factorization for Collaborative Filtering}

Neighbourhood methods, which compare users or items directly \cite{sarwar2001item}, gave way to latent-factor models after the Netflix Prize released about 100 million ratings \cite{bennett2007netflix}. Koren et al.\ showed that regularized matrix factorization outperforms neighbourhood approaches and scales to industrial data \cite{koren2009matrix}, and later models added neighbourhood and implicit signals to the same computational core \cite{koren2008factorization}. The model is trained mainly by stochastic gradient descent (SGD), which is cheap per update but inherently sequential, or by ALS \cite{zhou2008largescale}, which costs a Gram matrix and a Cholesky solve per entity but parallelizes naturally because each entity's subproblem is independent. ALS also extends to the confidence-weighted implicit-feedback objective of Hu et al.\ \cite{hu2008collaborative}, and its sweep count can be reduced by extrapolation, as shown for tensor decomposition \cite{ma2022pairwise,evans2023blockwise}; the momentum scheme of Section~\ref{sec:extended} applies the same principle to matrix factorization. We target explicit-feedback ALS because its dense per-entity subproblems expose structure that tensor cores can consume, and we evaluate on the Netflix and MovieLens \cite{harper2016movielens} benchmarks.

\subsection{Parallel and GPU-Accelerated Factorization}

On CPUs, Hogwild!\ runs SGD lock-free \cite{recht2011hogwild}, distributed SGD (DSGD) partitions the rating matrix across nodes \cite{gemulla2011large}, and CCD++ reformulates training as cache-friendly coordinate descent \cite{yu2012scalable}. On GPUs, the leading explicit-ALS system is cuMF\_als, which tiles Gram assembly through registers and shared memory \cite{tan2016faster}; it can store the system matrix of its conjugate-gradient (CG) solver in FP16 to save bandwidth but computes on CUDA cores. The same project provides cuMF\_SGD \cite{xie2017cumf} and cuMF\_CCD, the GPU CCD++ of Nisa et al.\ \cite{nisa2017parallel}, and Section~\ref{sec:external} compares against all three. Gates et al.\ accelerated implicit-feedback ALS with batched kernels and autotuning \cite{gates2015accelerating}; because that objective combines a dense Gram term shared by all users with a sparse per-user correction, Table~\ref{tab:litgap} marks it as partially sparse-aware. Chen et al.\ proposed BALS, a blocked ALS built on a tiled sparse format \cite{chen2021bals}, which serves as the storage baseline of our scalar path. As Volkov and Demmel showed, performance depends on matching kernel structure to the microarchitecture \cite{volkov2008benchmarking}, which on current GPUs includes tensor cores.

\subsection{Tensor Cores and Mixed-Precision Computing}

Tensor cores perform a $16 \times 16 \times 16$ matrix multiply--accumulate per warp-level instruction with FP16 inputs and FP32 accumulation. Their throughput, programmability, and rounding behaviour have been characterized by Markidis et al.\ \cite{markidis2018nvidia} and by microbenchmark studies through the Hopper generation \cite{jia2018dissecting,luo2024hopper}, and the WMMA interface is specified in the CUDA documentation \cite{nvidia2023cuda}. Blanchard et al.\ bounded the rounding error of the block fused multiply--add these units implement, showing that FP32 accumulation of FP16 products is far more accurate than pure FP16 arithmetic \cite{blanchard2020mixed}; Higham and Mary survey the mixed-precision algorithms built on such guarantees \cite{higham2002accuracy,higham2022mixed}. Mixed precision is established in deep learning \cite{micikevicius2018mixed} and in dense solvers, where tensor-core iterative refinement solves FP64 systems up to $4\times$ faster \cite{haidar2018harnessing} and least-squares problems admit several refinement strategies \cite{carson2025comparison}. Those methods recover a high-precision solution from a low-precision factorization; we ask instead whether the ALS normal equations tolerate low-precision \textit{formation} without refinement. For sparse computations, Zachariadis et al.\ dispatched dense sub-blocks of sparse matrix--matrix products to tensor cores \cite{zachariadis2020accelerating}, and recent systems refine this approach through data-movement optimization \cite{fan2024dtcspmm}, format and pipeline co-design \cite{zhao2025accspmm}, support for unstructured patterns \cite{okanovic2024unstructured}, and block-density transformations \cite{dong2026blocksmith}. Each optimizes a single sparse kernel in isolation; none embeds it in a training loop or reports the accuracy of the resulting model.

\subsection{Batched Small-Matrix Solvers}

Each ALS half-iteration produces hundreds of thousands of independent $K \times K$ symmetric positive-definite systems. Abdelfattah et al.\ analyzed the occupancy, register-pressure, and memory-layout trade-offs of factorizing many small matrices on GPUs \cite{abdelfattah2017factorization}, and Luszczek et al.\ proposed interfaces for batched sparse and mixed-precision solvers embedded in larger applications \cite{luszczek2024batched}. Our solver adapts the classical blocked right-looking factorization \cite{golub2013matrix} to the shared-memory capacity and synchronization model of a single streaming multiprocessor.

\subsection{Summary and Research Gap}

To our knowledge, existing GPU ALS systems compute on FP32 CUDA cores. No prior system trains explicit-feedback ALS on tensor cores with a degree-adaptive precision dispatch and shows, against an audited FP32 baseline under identical conditions, that the end-to-end accuracy cost is negligible.
